As if we didn’t already have enough dashboards, we had to go and invent “AI share of voice”.

Sigh. Yet another metric marketers have to be raked over the coals for.

Jokes aside, AI share of voice can be useful. It tells you how visible your brand is in AI responses compared with your competitors.

But the number is only as meaningful as the way it’s calculated.

So in this guide, I’ll show you exactly what AI share of voice is, how it’s calculated, how to measure it in Brand Radar, and how not to read more into the number than it can tell you.

AI share of voice (AI SOV) is the share of AI visibility your brand captures compared with the competitors you track, across the prompts you care about.

A common way to calculate it is:

AI SOV = responses that mention your brand ÷ responses that mention any tracked brand × 100

Let’s say you track three prompts about email marketing software on ChatGPT, and compare your brand with two competitors. Here’s which brands each response mentions:

Every brand appears in two of the three responses, so each has 2 ÷ 3 = 67%.

But that treats every prompt equally, and “best email marketing software” is asked far more often than the ecommerce automation question. Appearing in that first answer puts you in front of many more people.

So in Brand Radar, we weight each prompt by AI-adjusted volume: the monthly Google search volume of the keyword behind the prompt, multiplied by a platform ratio. The ratio reflects how much traffic each AI platform sends to websites compared with Google organic search, based on Ahrefs’ traffic data.

For ChatGPT, it’s currently 0.115, so a 20,000-search keyword becomes 2,300 on ChatGPT.

When a response mentions your brand (or cites your website), that prompt’s AI-adjusted volume counts toward your estimated impressions. Your AI share of voice is your impressions divided by the impressions of all responses that mention any tracked brand (here, 2,300 + 920 + 115 = 3,335):

Same responses, same brands, but your share drops to 31% because your mentions come from the prompts people ask least. Impressions are a modeled estimate of exposure, not a count of people who saw an answer.

Each platform gets its own ratio, because people use them very differently: currently 0.115 for ChatGPT, 0.011 for Gemini, and 0.01 for Perplexity. So the same 20,000-search prompt carries 2,300 impressions on ChatGPT but only 220 on Gemini.

We calculate AI SOV per platform, and when you combine platforms, the overall score is a weighted average where platforms with more impressions count for more.

For example, let’s say the same prompts give you 28% AI SOV on ChatGPT and 11% on Gemini. Because ChatGPT carries roughly ten times the impressions, your combined score lands at about 26.5%, which hides the Gemini weakness almost completely.

So check each platform on its own before looking at the combined number.

Every number above depends on choices: which prompts, platforms, and competitors you track, where, and when.

Change any of them and the percentage changes, which is also why two tools can report different AI SOV for the same brand without either being wrong.

Only the second tells you what the 24% actually refers to.

There isn’t a universally good AI share-of-voice score.

Think about it: If you compete against one other brand, 20% SOV means something very different than if you compete against nine brands.

Likewise, 20% from one prompt set isn’t automatically comparable with 20% from another tool using a different methodology.

Otherwise you’re comparing two percentages that happen to have the same symbol after them.

You checked your report last month: 20%. This month: 27%. Before you pop the champagne, work out which of three things moved it.

You tracked the same prompts, competitors, and platforms in both months, and your brand now appears in 95 of 300 responses instead of 60.

Because the setup didn’t change, that’s strong evidence your visibility genuinely improved. Maybe new content started getting picked up, or competitors lost ground.

If you added prompts, removed a competitor, or dropped a platform in between, you’re not making a like-for-like comparison (see step 7 above).

You changed the ruler, not your visibility. Document every scope change so you can tell the difference later.

Let’s say you track the prompt “What are the best SEO tools for a startup?” One week, ChatGPT says, “Ahrefs, Semrush, and Ubersuggest are strong options.” The next week, with no change to your setup, it says, “Semrush, Moz, and SE Ranking are worth considering.”

That doesn’t mean your SEO got worse in seven days. The model may have been updated, its retrieval may have pulled different sources, or the answer may simply have varied between runs.

Across hundreds of prompts, that noise adds up, so trust trends that persist across several reporting periods rather than one-off spikes.

AI share of voice will probably get more useful as AI search matures.

We’ll get better data, better sampling, better ways to estimate demand, and better ways to separate a passing mention from meaningful visibility.

But I don’t think the core problem will change.

The number will still depend on what you chose to measure.

So the real advantage won’t come from obsessing over who has 22% versus 24% SOV this week. It’ll come from building a measurement setup you trust, watching how it changes over time, and understanding why it changed.

That’s where AI share of voice becomes genuinely useful.

Not as another score to chase, but as an actual way to understand how your brand’s position in AI responses is evolving.
