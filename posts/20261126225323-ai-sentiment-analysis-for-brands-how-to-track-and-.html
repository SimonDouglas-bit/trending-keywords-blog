By Mateusz Makosiewicz

SEO & Marketing Educator at Ahrefs

AI sentiment analysis tracks whether AI assistants like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot describe your brand positively, negatively, or neutrally across many answers. It’s more of a market research tool, not a score to chase, at least in my opinion.

It can help you spot recurring objections, see what competitors get praised for, and find ways to improve your products and how you explain them. After all, AI visibility isn't just about whether your brand appears in AI answers. What AI actually says about you matters just as much.

In this guide, I’ll show you how to track praise and criticism in AI answers, investigate the pages AI cites, and decide what to act on. The aim is a more accurate, balanced description of your brand.

Treat what you find as clues to investigate alongside customer feedback, not a replacement for it.

When ChatGPT, Gemini, or another AI assistant talks about a brand, it doesn't necessarily repeat what one particular review or website says.

Depending on the assistant and how it's being used, it can draw on information learned during training, retrieve information from the web, and combine details from different sources into its own assessment.

This is why you'll sometimes see ChatGPT phrase recommendations as "What I like about [brand]" or "My biggest concern with [product]."

AI's "opinion" doesn't always appear in the original source. For example, a website might simply list a product's price. AI could turn that into "too expensive for small teams" after comparing it with alternatives. The underlying price is a fact, but the conclusion is an interpretation.

The question also makes a difference. Ask whether an iPhone is worth buying, and you might get a fairly balanced answer. Ask why iPhones are overpriced, and you're practically inviting the assistant to criticize them.

On top of that, AI answers can change between assistants, dates, and even repeated searches. A model update or a different selection of sources can shift the answer without anything changing about the product.

So I wouldn't treat one negative AI answer as proof that your brand has a reputation problem.

But when the same criticism keeps appearing across different answers, it's worth investigating. At the very least, you know it's something potential buyers might encounter.

And that's where sentiment analysis becomes useful.

AI sentiment analysis is a way to find out whether AI assistants describe your brand positively, negatively, or neutrally.

In practice, you're looking for more than a simple positive or negative label. You want to know what AI praises or criticizes, how often those opinions appear, and whether they reflect reality.

You can start without any special tools.

Open ChatGPT, Gemini, or whichever assistants your customers are likely to use, and ask questions such as:

Save the answers, along with any sources they cite.

Then paste the answers into a chatbot with an instruction like this:

For each remark about [brand], say whether it criticizes the brand, praises it, or only describes it. If you can’t tell, label it ambiguous.

Remember that one answer can contain several opinions. ChatGPT might praise Ahrefs for its features while also saying it's expensive. That's one positive remark and one negative remark in the same answer.

It's also worth reading the negative remarks yourself. AI is useful for sorting through large amounts of text, but it can misinterpret context or label a reasonable product trade-off as criticism.

If you're only checking a handful of answers, this manual approach is perfectly fine. But it becomes harder once you want to compare several AI assistants, track your competitors, and see whether things change over time.

That's where tools like Ahrefs Brand Radar come in. They collect answers to questions about your brand and industry, identify positive and negative remarks, and let you explore the patterns without reading every answer from scratch.

Here's how I'd approach it.

The trick is to start broad enough to discover unexpected opinions, then narrow your attention to the ones that actually matter.

I'll walk through this using Brand Radar, but the same principles apply if you're collecting the answers manually.

First, think about what customers actually ask AI about.

For Ahrefs, that's relatively straightforward. People often refer to the company and its tools by the same name, so tracking "Ahrefs" gives us a useful starting point.

Apple is a different story. Someone researching iPhone battery life probably isn't thinking about Apple's entire product lineup. And if you lump iPhone complaints together with praise for AirPods or MacBooks, the overall sentiment won't tell you much.

In that case, I'd track Apple as a brand but also monitor individual products.

Most companies fall somewhere in between: one main brand, a few important products, and perhaps some features that customers recognize by name.

Brand Radar lets you track several of these in one report.

I'd also add your closest competitors. Otherwise, you might discover that AI frequently criticizes your pricing without realizing it says the same thing about everyone in your category.

The comparison gives those opinions some much-needed context.

Next, you need to decide which questions to track.

I recommend using two types.

Provoked prompts deliberately explore criticisms you already know about.

These questions are useful because they let you investigate specific concerns. If customers frequently complain about repair costs, you can see how AI explains that problem and whether its information is accurate.

But there's a catch: you're asking for criticism, so you shouldn't be surprised when you get it.

That's why you also need unprovoked prompts: ordinary questions people might ask without mentioning your brand or asking for its drawbacks.

Think "best SEO tools," "best wireless earbuds," or "AI brand monitoring tools."

These are especially interesting because they reveal opinions AI brings into the conversation on its own.

Maybe an assistant recommends your product but warns that beginners might find it overwhelming. Or perhaps it praises a competitor for something your product also does well. Those are insights you might never find by asking only about your own brand.

In Brand Radar, I use tags to keep the two groups separate. I also tag prompts by product, so I can compare specific products without mixing everything together. That distinction matters when we get to the results.

Once you've collected enough answers, start with the big picture.

Brand Radar's Sentiment report shows how frequently AI makes positive and negative remarks about each brand. You can compare competitors, assistants, countries, and individual topics.

It's a useful way to spot differences that deserve a closer look.

For example, in one of our Apple reports, eight answers mentioned Apple negatively, while two also contained positive remarks. That produced a net sentiment of −75: 25% positive minus 100% negative.

The percentages don't have to add up to 100%, because an answer can contain both praise and criticism.

For a fairer competitor comparison, I prefer looking at unprovoked prompts that don't explicitly name any of the brands being compared.

In one filtered sample, Apple and Samsung appeared in similar shares of answers containing negative remarks. Samsung, however, appeared in positive answers about 18 percentage points more often.

That gives us a much more interesting question than "Which company has the better sentiment score?"

You can narrow the report further by platform, country, or topic. For example, what ChatGPT tells US customers about a newly launched product might matter far more to your business than what another assistant says about an older product in a smaller market.

You can also use the Aspect report, which groups opinions by topics such as pricing, reliability, and performance.

That helps separate different problems. After all, a brand that's frequently criticized for price but praised for reliability is in a very different position from one that's criticized for both.

Still, the charts are only the starting point.

The real insights usually appear when you read the actual answers.

In Brand Radar, you can open the full response and see the positive and negative remarks highlighted.

Read the surrounding text, not just the highlighted sentence. Is AI making a factual claim? Expressing an opinion? Comparing two products? Or describing a reasonable limitation for a particular customer?

Check whether the same issue appears across several answers or whether it's something the assistant mentioned once. By this point, you should have a shortlist of criticisms worth investigating.

Now comes the more interesting question: what can you actually do about them?

Before trying to fix a negative AI answer, check what needs changing: an outdated price needs a correction, a fair complaint may need a product improvement, and some answers just need context. Your response depends on the criticism and whether a source supports it:

We’ll focus on answers with cited sources we can inspect, starting with whether those pages support the criticism. The goal is to help AI describe your brand accurately and give buyers useful context.

Oftentimes, if the criticism is fair, there is very little you can do about it other than improving your product or service.

Start with the pages that appear in answers criticizing your brand.

In Brand Radar, open the Cited pages report and filter for negative sentiment. You can narrow the results by product, topic, or AI assistant.

I look for pages that appear repeatedly, particularly in answers from the assistants my customers are most likely to use.

Then I open the pages and check what they actually say. This step matters because a citation doesn't necessarily mean the page supports the criticism.

Maybe the author explicitly calls your product overpriced. But perhaps the article simply lists its price, and AI has reached that conclusion independently.

Or maybe the page compares an older version of your product, and AI doesn't make the distinction clear.

In other words, you need to separate what the source says from what AI concludes.

You can use AI to speed this up—dramatically speed this up.

I tested an approach using Letaido, Ahrefs Brand Radar, and Apify. I gave it 66 ChatGPT answers comparing PayPal and Square for small businesses in the US, collected between September 8 and November 13, 2026.

The analysis checked 538 negative claims against 184 pages listed in those answers.

The idea was to save the tedious work of opening sources one by one, finding the relevant passages, and comparing them with the claims in AI answers.

I've shared the prompt on GitHub if you'd like to try something similar.

You still need to review the results, of course. But once you've done that, you have something much more useful than a collection of negative labels: a clearer picture of which claims are supported, which are misleading, and where corrections might help.

Not every negative remark deserves your attention. I'd start with claims that appear frequently, could influence a buying decision, and leave you with something practical to do.

For example, when I investigated Apple's sentiment, I found more than 200 answers labeled negative that linked to two Trustpilot pages.

Opening those pages revealed unclaimed Apple profiles with poor ratings and hundreds of unanswered reviews.

That doesn't automatically mean the complaints are fair, but it gives Apple something concrete to investigate: what are customers complaining about, how many problems are legitimate, and which ones could be resolved or responded to?

I found another interesting example involving Ethical Consumer.

Its coverage of Apple's sustainability drew on older reporting from 2022, even though more recent information was available.

That doesn't make the original criticism wrong. But it raises a reasonable question: would the assessment change if the publisher considered the latest evidence?

For instance, if Apple's newer sustainability reporting shows improvements in recycled materials, that could be relevant to an article discussing its environmental performance.

The sensible next step would be to compare the claims with the new information and, where appropriate, suggest an update.

Notice that neither example involves asking someone to publish a more flattering opinion. One is about understanding and responding to customer complaints. The other is about making sure a publisher has up-to-date evidence.

Both could improve the information people encounter about Apple, whether through AI or directly on the web.

On your own website, that might mean updating a pricing page, help article, FAQ, or product comparison. This is worth checking even if the information seems obvious to you.

This is also part of generative engine optimization (GEO, aka AEO): making accurate, up-to-date information about your brand easier for AI assistants to find and use.

To illustrate, in our October 27, 2026 snapshot, 22 of the 67 answers Brand Radar labeled negative cited an Ahrefs or help-center page. That doesn't mean our content caused the criticism. But if our own pages appear in negative answers, I certainly want to see whether anything is outdated, confusing, or missing.

For third-party articles, you can contact the author with the relevant passage, explain what needs correcting, and provide evidence.

We've had some success with this. In our research on mistakes in AI answers, we contacted 26 authors about factual errors involving Ahrefs. Fourteen replied, and six made corrections we could verify.

You can use AI to help find public contact details and draft messages, although I'd still check everything before reaching out.

If you have several possible publishers to contact, SEO data can help you prioritize. Estimated organic traffic and Domain Rating can give you a rough sense of a page's reach.

If the criticism is fair, correcting articles won't make the problem disappear.

I have a personal example here. I drive a Nissan X-Trail e-POWER, and when I see AI criticize its highway fuel economy, I recognize the complaint from my own experience. Trust me, no amount of outreach will make that car use less fuel.

What can make a difference is improving the product. Nissan's newer e-POWER generation is designed to address that weakness, with improvements in efficiency, particularly at highway speeds. That's the kind of change that can eventually give AI, reviewers, and customers something new to talk about.

And when your product improves, you need to explain what changed. Publish the evidence, update your comparisons, and give reviewers a chance to assess the new version.

An old review doesn't become inaccurate just because you've released something better. But buyers should be able to find information about the improved product too.

Finally, sometimes the source is accurate, and AI simply interprets it poorly.

In that case, asking the publisher to correct something they never said won't help.

Instead, look for opportunities to explain the issue more clearly on your own pages, with evidence and useful comparisons. You might not be able to control the assistant's interpretation, but you can make the facts easier to find and understand.

After you've corrected a page or contacted a publisher, you'll naturally want to know whether it made a difference.

First, check whether the page was actually updated. Then revisit the AI answers that originally raised the issue. Does AI still repeat the same criticism? Has it picked up the new information? Or does it simply no longer mention the issue?

You probably won't get a clear answer from checking once. AI responses vary, so it's better to look at results over time.

I've built a simple tracker for this using Letaido and Apify. It checks contacted pages for updates every three days and follows the same claims in new AI answers, even when those pages are no longer cited.

That lets me see whether the information changed and whether AI's answers followed. And of course, it saves a ton of time, helping you with a process that is not fun to do manually, to say the least.

For my Square analysis, the initial research and tracker setup cost an estimated $2.36–$2.57, excluding test runs. Rechecking the 24 flagged pages every three days and analyzing new answers was estimated to cost around $0.34 a month.

Of course, a corrected page doesn't guarantee a corrected AI answer. But at least you can see what's happening rather than guessing.

What if AI criticizes your brand but doesn't link to any supporting sources?

You can't reliably tell where the opinion came from, but you can still look for similar claims elsewhere on the web.

I'd start with reviews, Reddit discussions, and publishers with AI partnerships.

You can search manually or use our new Ahrefs Mentions Explorer to find negative mentions and recurring complaints. You can also see which pages are likely more influential based on our proprietary score and SEO metrics like domain rating or website traffic.

Its Insights report can also group those complaints into common themes.

Say ChatGPT repeatedly calls your product difficult to learn. You might discover that older reviews describe an onboarding process you've since improved.

Updating those reviews, publishing clearer information, or inviting reviewers to assess the current product could help in two ways.

First, there's a chance the improved information will influence future AI answers. Assistants that search the web may encounter the updates, and future models may draw on newer information. There's no guarantee, especially when you don't know what originally informed the answer.

Second, you're improving what potential customers see outside AI. Even if ChatGPT never changes its opinion, people reading those reviews or researching your product elsewhere will have more accurate information.

And sometimes you'll discover a genuine complaint that needs attention, not a correction.

That's why I think investigating uncited claims is still worthwhile. You may not be able to trace or directly change the AI answer, but you can improve the information and experiences that shape your brand's reputation.

AI sentiment analysis is most useful when you stop treating positive and negative mentions as scores and start treating them as clues.

Some will lead you to outdated information you can correct. Others will reveal genuine product problems or opportunities to explain your strengths better.

Not every action will change what AI says. But improving the information and experiences behind those opinions can still make a difference wherever people research your brand. And that's a much better outcome than simply chasing a higher sentiment score.
